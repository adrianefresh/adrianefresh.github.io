WHAT I STUDY

My research examines the deep structural forces that shape political systems and social hierarchies. I combine historical analysis, quantitative methods, and computational approaches to understand how institutions persist, change, and influence patterns of inequality. I am particularly interested in the intersection of economic change, political power, and social control across different historical periods and contexts.

Elite Persistence
How do elites remain powerful when the world around them changes? I study how economic and political elites adapt across generations and through structural transformation—and how their adaptations allow old hierarchies to persist under new conditions.

Institutional Change
What drives institutions to expand or contract access and opportunity? I study moments of institutional reform— from voting rights expansions to electoral administration changes—to understand the conditions under which institutions become more or less inclusive. My work explores how institutions can simultaneously expand formal rights while maintaining substantive inequality through alternative mechanisms.
Status Threat
& Social Control
When do challenges to social hierarchies become demands for greater social control? How do elites deploy institutions to maintain positions of advantage? And how do citizens interact with the state's social control institutions? I study how changes to the social order and perceived status threat shape policing, punishment, and incarceration. My research in this domain is especially interested in racial group status and carceral institutions in the United States.
Meaning in Social Scientific Research
How do we study causes whose meaning depends on how people interpret and interact with one another? I develop frameworks for causal inference about interpretive domains like conversations and social constructs, asking how observational variation and experimental interventions connect to the phenomena our theories describe. I also study how scientific judgments—particularly the appeal of surprising findings—shape what we as scientists ask and what we learn.
