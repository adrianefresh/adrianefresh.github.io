What I Study

My research examines the deep structural forces that shape political systems and social hierarchies. I combine historical analysis, quantitative methods, and computational approaches to understand how institutions persist, change, and influence patterns of inequality. I am particularly interested in the intersection of economic change, political power, and social control across different historical periods and contexts.

01
Elite Persistence
How do economic and political elites maintain their power across generations and through periods of significant structural change? I examine historical cases where rapid economic transformation might have been expected to disrupt existing hierarchies, yet elites found ways to adapt and sustain their dominance. This work bridges historical narrative with quantitative analysis to understand the mechanisms of elite stability.
02
Institutional Change
What drives institutions to expand or contract access and opportunity? I study moments of institutional reform— from voting rights expansions to electoral administration changes—to understand the conditions under which institutions become more or less inclusive. My work explores how institutions can simultaneously expand formal rights while maintaining substantive inequality through alternative mechanisms.
03
Status Threat & Social Control
How do threats to established hierarchies trigger policy responses and changes in social control? I investigate the relationship between demographic change, perceived status threat, and shifts in criminal justice policy, incarceration rates, and policing practices. This research illuminates how systems of inequality adapt when their foundations are challenged.
04
Meaning in Social Scientific Research
How do we meaningfully extract insights from data about social systems? Beyond technical questions of measurement and inference, I explore how we construct and interpret causal stories in the social sciences. This methodological work examines the role of conversational and discursive processes in shaping how we understand social phenomena and build scientific knowledge.
