excerpt: "<p style=\"margin-bottom: 0.8em;\"><em>Creating Contestation: Adversarial Politics and Institutional Change in Early Modern England</em> asks where one of the defining features of modern elections—public, adversarial political choice—came from. For much of England's early parliamentary history, representatives were not selected through competitive elections in the modern sense. Instead, candidates, patrons, and other local elites frequently bargained over who would be returned to Parliament, with the electorate largely affirming choices already made. During the long seventeenth century, these arrangements began to break down and contested elections became increasingly common.</p>

<p style=\"margin-bottom: 0.8em;\">I argue that this transformation cannot be understood simply as the electoral expression of growing social or ideological cleavages. Many contests instead emerged from failures of coordination among political elites, particularly conflicts among patrons whose authority to nominate representatives had previously rested on relatively stable hierarchies, relationships, and expectations. The book investigates why those arrangements became harder to sustain, examining changes in economic power, religion, Crown–elite relations, the value of parliamentary office, and beliefs about hierarchy, deference, and the legitimacy of open political conflict.</p>

<p>The book uses this history to develop a broader account of how decentralized, informal institutions change. Institutional theories often identify conditions under which one arrangement should replace another without explaining how that transition actually unfolds across many separate interactions. I distinguish between the breakdown of an established practice within particular political relationships and the subsequent process through which new behavior spreads, becomes expected, and sometimes acquires institutional regularity. The English case is especially revealing because this transformation was neither linear nor irreversible: electoral contestation expanded dramatically, later receded, and eventually became widespread again under different political and social conditions. By tracing these changes from individual electoral disputes to broader patterns of political behavior, the book seeks to explain not only the origins of electoral competition, but the process through which institutions are destabilized, reconstructed, generalized—and sometimes reversed.</p>"
