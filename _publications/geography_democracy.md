<div style="font-size: 0.9em; line-height: 1.6; color: #555;">
Democratic reforms are often studied as moments of franchise expansion, but they
can also reconstruct the geography through which political voice is represented.
This paper examines the construction and consequences of representative geogra-
phy in England’s Great Reform Act of 1832. I assemble new data linking changing
constituency boundaries to stable local geographies, pre-reform political conditions,
elections, and parliamentary careers. I show that population strongly structured the
redistribution of representation, while patronal power shaped the precise form that
new political geography took. Geographic reorganization increased electoral compe-
tition and sharply reduced the immediate persistence of incumbent MPs. Yet many
incumbents later returned, often through different constituencies, especially those
with more geographically portable political resources. The findings suggest that de-
mocratization can reshape representative institutions and political competition even
when elite turnover remains limited.
