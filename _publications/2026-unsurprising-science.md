<div style="font-size: 0.75em; line-height: 1.6; color: #555;">
Surprise is often considered a virtue of science.  But surprising science is not necessarily the most informative science.  This article contends that the epistemic value of surprising findings depends crucially on the warrant behind the beliefs about the prior state of knowledge against which new findings are evaluated.  While some beliefs are warranted by strong evidentiary foundations, others have come to be *taken as known* despite poor foundations.  When researchers allocate their finite inquiry resources towards questions *expected* to generate surprise, they neglect and discount questions whose answers would improve confidence in the warrant of existing knowledge.
