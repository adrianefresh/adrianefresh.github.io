This paper considers the persistence of Britain's 17th-century dramatic actors and their prevalence in subsequent eras. I leverage newly compiled data on the careers and outcomes of actors documented in the Elizabethan and Stuart theatres to examine whether those who were privileged participants in the theatrical world of the 1600s experienced greater social and political mobility. Specifically, I investigate whether thespian networks created in the theatrical sphere translated into political power and whether individuals born to theatrical families held advantages in access to political office. The analysis explores how cultural prominence and theatrical participation correlated with later political representation and suggests that cultural participation may serve as a pathway to political power—or conversely, that political families invested in cultural prominence as part of their elite strategy.
